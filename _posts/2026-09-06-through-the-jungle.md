Nearly two years have passed since my last entry here. Much has changed. Looking back at that post, I can see signs along the path that I couldn’t fully recognize at the time.

I wrote about resilience, adaptability, and finding ways to deliver under pressure. Those lessons were real. So was the strain behind them. What I described as professional growth was also an effort to make sense of being repeatedly asked to succeed without the scope, support, or resources the work required.

## When the challenge becomes the operating model

Difficult projects are part of consulting. Requirements change, environments surprise us, and even a well-planned engagement can become complicated. Creative problem-solving belongs in this work.

The problem comes when that creativity becomes the plan.

Over the last few years, I found myself responsible for delivering projects that were underscoped or sold with expectations the delivery team wasn’t equipped to meet. The commitments had been made, but the time, staffing, and support needed to fulfill them were often missing. Leadership expected successful outcomes, while much of the responsibility for closing those gaps fell to me.

I cared about the customers. I cared about the quality of the work and the people doing it. That made it difficult to step back from an unreasonable expectation. There was always another approach to investigate, another conversation to have, another way I might be able to make it work.

That effort carried a cost. The stress came from being accountable for outcomes while having limited control over the conditions that determined them.

## What I understand differently now

At the time, I often treated those situations as tests of my ability. If I could find the right approach, communicate more clearly, or work through one more obstacle, perhaps I could get the project where it needed to go.

Sometimes I could. But getting something across the finish line didn’t mean the assignment had been reasonable. It didn’t mean the approach was sustainable, either.

A successful recovery can hide the conditions that made recovery necessary. When people repeatedly compensate for gaps in planning and support, those gaps can start to look manageable from a distance. The outcome is visible. The strain required to achieve it is much easier to miss.

I can’t speak to every intention behind the decisions that put me in those situations. I can speak to their effect: I felt pressure to deliver without enough practical help to make success achievable.

I also see a distinction now that I didn’t always make then. A stretch assignment should give someone room to develop, access to guidance, and a realistic chance to succeed. Without those things, the challenge can become an exercise in endurance.

## Why this matters in security services

Security consulting requires judgment, technical depth, and trust. Customers deserve commitments that reflect the work involved. Delivery teams need the time and resources to understand an environment, evaluate tradeoffs, and carry out that work responsibly.

A project doesn’t become properly scoped because someone is willing to absorb the difference.

Creative solutions are valuable when they address a customer’s problem. They become exhausting when their primary purpose is to compensate for commitments that should have been questioned earlier.

I still believe in ownership. I believe in raising risks early, proposing alternatives, and doing the work to move a project forward. But ownership needs to exist at every level—including where promises are made, resources are assigned, and decisions about scope are approved.

## A different beginning

My experience at my new company has given me a different point of reference.

I’ve been given time to learn the business, understand how the organization operates, and invest in my own growth. There has been room to ask questions and build context before being expected to have every answer.

That has meant more to me than I initially expected.

Previously, the experience sometimes felt like having my hands and feet tied, being tossed into the ocean, and then being asked why I wasn’t swimming faster. Having time to learn and prepare has reminded me how much the starting conditions matter.

Learning the business is part of becoming useful to it. Understanding the people, processes, and expectations creates a foundation for better decisions and stronger delivery. It also gives me space to be curious again, rather than approaching every unfamiliar situation as the next emergency I need to solve.

I still want challenging work. I want to contribute, take responsibility, and keep developing. I’m learning how different that can feel when preparation and support are part of the expectation.

## Finding a clearer path

“Through the Jungle” feels like the right title for this chapter. For a long time, I was focused on clearing whatever stood immediately in front of me. There wasn’t much room to ask whether the path itself made sense.

Distance has helped.

I’m proud of the work I did, the customers I supported, and the lessons I earned. I can recognize those accomplishments while also acknowledging that some of the conditions were unhealthy for me. I don’t need to turn every difficult experience into a positive one to learn from it.

Going forward, I want to bring that perspective into how I deliver work and support others: clearer commitments, earlier conversations about risk, realistic expectations, and room to learn.

Resilience will always matter. I’m grateful to be building on a foundation that gives it room to become something more than survival.
